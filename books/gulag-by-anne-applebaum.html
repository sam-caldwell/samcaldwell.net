What Applebaum Set Out to Do


Anne Applebaum’s Gulag: A History is a big, archive-driven narrative of the Soviet forced-labor camp system—from its early Bolshevik roots through Stalin’s expansion, the postwar years, and the slow unraveling that followed his death. Her main pitch is that the Gulag wasn’t just a sidebar to Soviet history: it was a governing tool, an economic institution, and a social reality that shaped the USSR for decades. Applebaum writes as a historian, but she keeps returning to the human scale—how arrests happened, what transportation looked like, what people ate, how work was organized, how punishment worked, and what it took to survive. (Applebaum, 2003)

This makes the book a natural companion to Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn’s The Gulag Archipelago, which is less a conventional “history” and more a sweeping “literary investigation” built from personal experience and a vast body of testimony. Where Solzhenitsyn builds a moral and political indictment from lived suffering, Applebaum tries to map the whole system—its bureaucracy, timelines, regions, economic aims, and institutional logic—using newly accessible archives and scholarship. (Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974; Applebaum, 2003)

The Gulag’s Beginnings: Revolution, Civil War, and “Special Camps”


Applebaum starts early, emphasizing that the Gulag didn’t appear out of nowhere under Stalin. The Bolsheviks’ civil-war emergency politics helped normalize coercion, political repression, and the idea that “enemies” could be isolated and exploited through forced labor. Over time, the state developed more standardized ways to remove people from normal legal protections and place them into camp-like institutions.

A key early symbol is the Solovetsky camp (often shortened to “Solovki”), which became a kind of prototype: geographically remote, administratively experimental, and ideologically justified as “re-education through labor.” In Solzhenitsyn’s telling, these early camps help establish the “archipelago” metaphor—separate islands of captivity that nonetheless form a connected system across the Soviet landscape. Applebaum treats Solovki similarly as an early node where practices harden: the blending of punishment, political control, and economic extraction. (Applebaum, 2003; Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974).

Building a System: Bureaucracy, Quotas, and a Culture of Arrest


As the camps expand, Applebaum stresses the administrative reality: the Gulag becomes an institution with internal rules, reporting lines, transport networks, camp categories, and a steady flow of paperwork. It also becomes deeply entangled with policing and political campaigns. Arrests are not merely responses to crimes; they are often driven by ideological categories, paranoia, and incentives inside the security apparatus.

This is where the contrast with The Gulag Archipelago can be especially useful. Solzhenitsyn is famous for the arrest scenes—the sudden knock, the panic, the absurdity of interrogations, the sense that anyone can be taken. Applebaum doesn’t contradict this; she contextualizes it by showing how mass operations, political directives, and institutional pressures turned arrest into a kind of production process. Solzhenitsyn gives you the lived terror; Applebaum shows you how the machinery scaled. (Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974; Applebaum, 2003)

Stalin-Era Explosion: Camps as a Tool of Rule


The Stalin period is the book’s central gravity. The Gulag swells alongside collectivization, political purges, and rapid industrialization. Applebaum’s core argument is that the camp system becomes a multi-purpose instrument: it removes “unreliable” people, terrorizes society into compliance, and supplies labor for ambitious projects in inhospitable places.

One recurring theme is the way ideology and opportunism reinforce each other. The state can claim it is “cleansing” society and reforming criminals through work, while simultaneously using prisoners to dig canals, fell timber, mine gold, build railways, and populate remote regions. Applebaum highlights the constant tension between grand plans and brutal reality: coerced labor is often inefficient, wildly wasteful, and catastrophically lethal—yet it persists because it serves political ends and because institutions develop momentum. (Applebaum, 2003)

Publishers and reviewers often summarize the scale in staggering terms; for example, Publishers Weekly describes “nearly 30 million” people passing through Soviet labor camps over the decades, underscoring the breadth of the phenomenon even if precise totals are debated across sources. (Applebaum, 2003; Publishers Weekly, 2003)

The Camp World: Transport, Hunger, Work, Punishment, and Survival


One of Applebaum’s strengths is her detailed reconstruction of daily life. She describes the journey into the camp world: overcrowded prisons, transit points, and long transport by rail or ship. Arrival doesn’t mean stability; it often means reshuffling—between camp divisions, work brigades, punishment cells, and medical barracks.

Daily existence is organized around scarcity and coercion:

  • Food as control: Rations are tied to labor output, so hunger becomes a management tool. If you’re weak, you work less; if you work less, you eat less; the cycle can be fatal.
  • Work norms and quotas: “Norms” determine whether you get a survivable ration. This turns the workday into a constant negotiation—between effort, exhaustion, weather, tools that don’t work, and guards who don’t care.
  • Hierarchy among prisoners: Criminal prisoners often occupy a different status than political prisoners, and camp authorities sometimes manipulate these divisions. Informers, foremen, and privileged positions can mean the difference between life and death.
  • Punishment and terror: Solitary confinement, reduced rations, humiliations, arbitrary violence, and the ever-present threat of additional sentences keep prisoners off-balance.

Here again, Applebaum and Solzhenitsyn interlock. The Gulag Archipelago is relentless about the moral corrosion: what happens to a society when betrayal is rewarded, when truth is dangerous, when survival can depend on cruelty. Applebaum’s tone is more documentary, but the cumulative effect is similar: she shows how a system built on deprivation and fear reliably produces degradation—while also making space for the ways prisoners formed friendships, improvised culture, traded skills, and sometimes held onto decency. (Applebaum, 2003; Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974)

War and Postwar: The Gulag as a Wartime and Imperial Tool


World War II doesn’t “pause” the Gulag; it changes its inputs and functions. The state faces massive upheaval and manpower needs, and the camp system adapts. Applebaum describes new categories of prisoners, shifting security obsessions, and the incorporation of populations from newly controlled territories. The Gulag also remains tied to the extraction economy—especially in remote regions where free labor is hard to attract.

Solzhenitsyn’s narrative often frames the Gulag as a core feature of the Soviet regime’s moral logic—war or peace, the system continues because it is politically useful. Applebaum complements this by showing the bureaucratic continuity: the same institutional habits—classification, quotas, secrecy, administrative coercion—persist through changing historical circumstances. (Applebaum, 2003; Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974)

After Stalin: Reform, Release, and the System’s Slow Retreat


Stalin’s death becomes a turning point, but not an instant ending. Applebaum emphasizes the unevenness: amnesties, reviews of cases, and political shifts lead to releases, yet the camp and prison system does not vanish overnight. Some structures remain, and the logic of isolating “dangerous” or “undesirable” people persists, even as the most expansive, mass-terror version of the Gulag loses legitimacy.

This is where cross-referencing Solzhenitsyn is especially clarifying. The Gulag Archipelago famously centers on the period 1918–1956 in its subtitle, treating 1956 (and the Khrushchev-era thaw) as a symbolic boundary for that specific historical system. Applebaum likewise treats the post-Stalin years as a phase of contraction and transformation rather than a clean break. (Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974; Applebaum, 2003)

Memory, Denial, and What the Gulag Did to Society


Applebaum closes with a question that’s less administrative and more cultural: what happens after a society runs an institution like this for decades? One answer is damaged trust. When huge numbers of people have been arrested, coerced into informing, or forced to navigate a world where words can destroy you, the social fabric doesn’t bounce back quickly.

Here, Applebaum and Solzhenitsyn converge in spirit even if their methods differ. Solzhenitsyn is explicitly moral: he wants readers to see how totalitarian systems recruit ordinary people into complicity, and he treats memory as a civic duty. Applebaum, writing later and with more documentary access, arrives at a similar conclusion: without honest public reckoning, the habits of repression can linger—especially in institutions and in the stories people tell (or don’t tell) about their past. (Applebaum, 2003; Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974)

Why the Applebaum–Solzhenitsyn Pairing Works


If you read Gulag: A History alongside The Gulag Archipelago, you get a fuller picture:

  • Solzhenitsyn supplies the internal experience and the ethical force—how it felt, what it did to souls, how the logic of repression spread through everyday life. (Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974)
  • Applebaum supplies the external map—how the system evolved over time, how it was administered, and how it intersected with the Soviet economy and state-building. (Applebaum, 2003)

They don’t always emphasize the same things, but they reinforce a shared bottom line: the Gulag was not an accident or a mere collection of isolated abuses. It was an enduring state system—one that shaped politics, economics, culture, and millions of lives. (Applebaum, 2003; Solzhenitsyn, 1973/1974)
